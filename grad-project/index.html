Graduate Final Project

CS 485/585 • Fall 2026 • University of New Mexico

< back to syllabus

Weight: 30% of your overall course grade
Team Size: Four (4) students
Format: Multiple milestones throughout the semester, see below

For the final project, teams of four students will design and carry out a feasibility study. You will investigate whether a networking-related idea, approach, or intervention actually works, grounded in both existing literature and evidence you collect yourselves. Example project tasks include running an experiment, building a prototype, or taking measurements.

Choosing Your Topic

Your topic can be almost anything connected to networking. Examples include a technical implementation, a measurement study, or a community-facing prototype. There is no fixed menu of approved topics.

Your final paper should address the following components:

  1. Technical feasibility. Describe what you built, measured, or tested, and the data or materials it required.
  2. Economic feasibility. Describe the cost of your project in money, time, or equipment, how you used your budget, and any adjustments you made based on what you had available.
  3. Operational feasibility. Describe the population or community your project would serve and whether it meets a real need for that population.
  4. Evaluation. Describe how you assessed whether your approach worked, including what success or failure looked like and how you measured it.

Replicating an existing study. This is a good project type, but it needs a clear reason. Running tests in New Mexico may produce a meaningfully different result, or your team may change a variable, a dimension, or bring in a new perspective the original study did not consider.

Data Collection

Every team is required to collect data as follows.

A literature review. Find out what is already known. Has someone tried this before? Is there an organization already doing it? Does a paper's approach still hold up, or has something changed that makes it obsolete? Document this as an annotated bibliography, due as part of your proposal and updated as your project develops.

Your own collected evidence. A literature review alone is not sufficient. Run an experiment, build and test a prototype, take real measurements, or otherwise generate original evidence of your own. Use this data, even if preliminary or partial, to support your claims about what worked, what did not, and why.

A strong final paper connects these two ideas. It states what you now believe about the problem, based on what has been tried before and on what you tried yourselves.

Scope and Ethics Boundary

This course does not have IRB approval for human-subjects research.

You may not recruit outside participants, run studies with community members, or collect data from anyone outside your team as part of this class.

Within this boundary, there are two things you can and should do.

  • You may build, test, and evaluate your work within your own team. Team members testing a prototype on each other, or evaluating a system using their own usage data, is acceptable.
  • Secondary research is encouraged. Read what others have found, see how similar organizations or projects have approached a similar problem, and identify open questions or gaps.

If your project idea grows out of something you are already involved in, like volunteer work, a community connection, or a problem you have encountered elsewhere, that is a great source of motivation. If, after the semester, you want to pursue the idea further with real participants, talk to your instructor.

Teach Us Something New

Your final presentation should teach the class something you learned in the process. A tutorial or demo that only walks through what you built is not enough.

That insight might look like:

  • Discovering that an organization or project already does what you set out to build, and what that tells you.
  • Finding that an approach from the literature no longer holds up, and why.
  • Learning that your problem was harder, or easier, than you expected, and what that reveals.
  • An unexpected result from your own data that changes how you would frame the problem.

Individual Reflection

In addition to your team's final paper, each student will individually complete a short, private Canvas quiz. Only the instructor will read your responses. This is not a place to perform, it is a space to reflect honestly on how the semester went, including how your team worked together.

The quiz will include questions along these lines.

  1. Reflect on your experience with your feasibility study this semester. What did you get out of it, and what was rewarding or hard? (5-10 sentences)
  2. How much work did you personally put into the project? Be honest about your time and effort, what you are proud of, and where you fell short. (5-10 sentences)
  3. How did each of your teammates contribute to the project? This response is confidential and only the instructor will read it. (3-5 sentences)
  4. Is there anything else you would like the instructor to know? (3-5 sentences, or write N/A if there is nothing to add)

This quiz is graded on completion. Please do not share your responses with your teammates.

AI Use Policy

LLM use is permitted for this project, provided it enhances rather than replaces your own learning and thinking. You must disclose which tools you used and at what stage.

Your final paper should include a short AI reflection addressing:

  • How did you decide when to use an LLM, and when not to?
  • If you used one, when did you disagree with something it suggested, and what did you do instead?
  • If you did not use an LLM, how do you think your project would have gone differently if you had?
  • How did your use of AI, or your choice not to use it, support or get in the way of your own learning?
