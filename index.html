Course Overview

This class is a theoretical and practical study of computer networks: how they're structured, how protocols work together, how data finds its way from one place to another reliably and efficiently. You'll work through the core layers of the internet, from applications down to physical infrastructure, and get hands-on practice with sockets, packet analysis, and network programming.

Alongside the technical material, the course looks at where networks physically operate and who they do or don't serve. New Mexico's broadband landscape, including rural and tribal connectivity, is used throughout the semester to ground abstract concepts in real infrastructure and real communities. Most Tuesdays sessions are discussion-based, working through both classic networking papers and more applied readings; Thursdays are more traditional lectures with in-class exercises.

Learning Objectives

All students:

  • Explain the layered architecture of networks and the role of protocols at each layer
  • Analyze tradeoffs in network design, such as reliability vs. efficiency or wired vs. wireless
  • Connect networking concepts to real, local lived experiences, including questions of access and the digital divide
  • Engage with current issues, emerging research, and ongoing debates in the networking field

Graduate students, additionally:

  • Scope an independent group project at the intersection of networking and a domain of their choosing
  • Teach the class a new concept or skill through a final project presentation

Texts & Readings

Primary Text

Kurose & Ross, Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach (8th or 9th edition).

You are responsible for securing access to the primary textbook. Please check the Calendar for the most up-to-date information on required chapters/sections and due dates.

In addition to the textbook, we will be discussing relevant research papers throughout the semester. These will be provided to you as PDFs or links to the original sources. See a summary list of the papers below.

Foundational Readings

  • "End-to-End Arguments in System Design." Jerome H. Saltzer, David P. Reed & David D. Clark. ACM Transactions on Computer Systems (TOCS), 1984.
  • "Tussle in Cyberspace: Defining Tomorrow's Internet." David D. Clark, John Wroclawski, Karen R. Sollins & Robert Braden. ACM SIGCOMM, 2002.

Applied Readings

  • "Mending the Fabric: The Contentious, Collaborative Work of Repairing Broadband Maps." Beatriz Palacios Abad, Elizabeth Belding, Morgan Vigil-Hayes & Ellen Zegura. ACM CSCW, 2024.
  • "Network Capacity as Common Pool Resource: Community-Based Congestion Management in a Community Network." Matthew William Johnson, Esther Han Beol Jang, Frankie O'Rourke, Rachel Ye & Kurtis Heimerl. ACM CSCW, 2021.
  • "Anyone, Anywhere, not Everyone, Everywhere: Starlink Doesn't End the Digital Divide." Wesley Woo, Juan A. Fraire, Sylvia Ratnasamy, Scott Shenker & Shaddi Hasan. ACM HotNets, 2025.

Succeeding in This Class

The points below describe, in general terms, what it takes to do well in this course.

  • Consistency: Students who keep up with weekly homework and readings will find the workload steady and manageable. The lowest two homework grades are dropped.
  • Technical Competency: Students who write clear, well-tested code and can explain the design choices behind it will do well on labs.
  • In-Class Engagement: We will use an on-call system for discussion days. You will be assigned to Group 1 or Group 2 during Week 2, and each group is responsible for actively contributing on its assigned discussion days. Everyone is expected to come prepared regardless of group, but on-call students are specifically expected to speak up.
  • Independent Inquiry & Collaboration (graduate students): Graduate students who start scoping project ideas early, draw on the optional readings list for inspiration, and treat discussion days as a chance to workshop ideas and get feedback will be in the strongest position going into the proposal.

Late Work Policy

  • Technical Issues: Problems with GitHub, Canvas, or similar tools are not penalized, but you need to report them as soon as they happen, not after the deadline has passed.
  • Extenuating Circumstances: Life happens. If something comes up during the semester, reach out as early as you can so we can work out a reasonable path forward together.
  • Homework: Homework assignments are due at 11:59pm on Mondays. You will have a grace period until 10:59am on Tuesday for up to 80% credit. No late submissions accepted after the grace period. Two lowest scores dropped.
  • Labs: 10% per day penalty. Late submissions accepted up to a week late. Contact me before the deadline if you anticipate needing more time.
  • Quizzes & Exams: Given in class, on paper. No makeups except for documented emergencies or university-approved absences, arranged in advance whenever possible.
  • On-Call Discussion Days: If you must miss your group's on-call day, you may make it up on another discussion day, but you must notify me and your TA in advance.
  • Project Proposal & Milestones (grad students): 10% per day penalty. Late submissions accepted up to a week late. Contact me before the deadline if you anticipate needing more time. Please note that a late Proposal submission may result in issues for subsequent Milestones, try to submit this on time.
  • Group Presentation and Final Paper (grad students): Due dates are firm given end-of-semester scheduling. Extensions are rare and must be requested at least one week in advance.

Attendance Policy

Attendance is mandatory on the days your group is on-call for discussion. Beyond that, I will not track attendance. However, this is a largely discussion-driven course. Showing up regularly is in your own interest.

My goal is to make this a class you find worthwhile to attend. If you are curious about a specific topic, bring it up on a discussion day, or sign up for a show-and-tell slot for extra credit.

Devices in Class

I recommend you take handwritten notes. Please do not use laptops or phones during class unless you are specifically told to. If a particular class session needs a device, you will be told in advance. Quizzes and exams will be given entirely on paper. If you have a specific need for an exception to this policy, please ask.

Use of Generative AI

Use of generative AI is discouraged (except when an assignment explicitly asks for it), but it is not prohibited. You will be asked to disclose your use (or non-use) of AI in every assignment.

All work you submit must be your own. You should never include in your assignment anything that was not written directly by you without proper citation (including quotation marks and in-line citation for direct quotes). If you entire assignment is attributed to a source other than yourself, you will not get a good grade.

Including anything you did not write in your assignment without proper citation or including fake citations will be treated as an academic misconduct case and may result in a zero on the assignment or a failing grade in the course.
